## The universal acceptance checklist

::: {.checklist}
**Before any assessment or AI interview**

- [ ] Read every instruction twice. Most failures are instruction-following failures, not knowledge failures.
- [ ] Block 60–90 uninterrupted minutes. Turn off notifications and close other tabs.
- [ ] Use a laptop or desktop (not a phone), a stable connection, a working webcam and mic, and good front lighting.
- [ ] Have your resume open. AI interviewers ask about it.
- [ ] Prepare 3 project stories (2 minutes each) with numbers.
- [ ] Do not use ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grammarly, or translation tools *during* the assessment. Platforms detect it and will remove you.
- [ ] Write in complete sentences. Show your reasoning, not just your answer.
- [ ] Proofread once before submitting.
:::

## Question categories you will face

| Category | Example | What graders look for |
|---|---|---|
| **Instruction following** | "Answer in exactly 3 bullet points under 50 words." | Every constraint met |
| **Response comparison** | "Which response is better? Explain." | Correct choice, specific reasons tied to a rubric |
| **Fact-checking** | "Identify all errors in this paragraph." | Finding subtle errors; not inventing false ones |
| **Writing an ideal response** | "Write the response an expert assistant should give." | Accuracy, completeness, tone, format |
| **Safety judgment** | "Should the assistant comply with this request?" | Recognizing harm; helpful refusal or safe alternative |
| **Domain depth** (expert roles) | "Explain why this tax treatment is wrong." | Real expertise, precise terms |
| **Coding** | "Find the bug; write tests." | Correct diagnosis, clean fix, edge cases |
| **Rater guidelines** | "Rate this search result's relevance (Fully Meets → Fails to Meet)." | Consistency with the guideline definitions |
| **Resume deep-dive** (AI interview) | "Walk me through your role on [project]." | Specifics, ownership, results |
| **Behavioral** (employers) | "Tell me about a time you handled an angry customer." | STAR structure, empathy, outcome |

## Mock Assessment 1: Compare two AI responses

*These mock items are original practice material. They do not reproduce any platform's real test.*

::: {.sample}
**User prompt:** "Explain compound interest to a 12-year-old in under 100 words, with one example."

> **Response A (148 words):** "Compound interest is when the interest you earn gets added to your principal, and then future interest is calculated on the new, larger principal. This is different from simple interest, which is only calculated on the original amount. Financial institutions use an annual percentage yield (APY) to show this. For example, if you deposit $100 at 10% interest compounded annually, after one year you have $110. In year two, you earn 10% of $110, which is $11, so you have $121. Over long time horizons, compounding can significantly increase the value of savings, which is why starting early matters…"

> **Response B (82 words):** "Imagine your money is a snowball rolling downhill. The bank pays you a little extra money, called interest, for keeping your money there. Next time, it pays interest on your money *plus* the interest you already earned, so the snowball grows faster. Example: put $100 in a bank that pays 10% each year. After one year you have $110. After two years you have $120. The longer you wait, the bigger it gets!"

**Task:** Which response is better? Explain in 3–5 sentences.
:::

::: {.qa}
**Strong answer:** *Response A is better, though both have problems.* Response B has a factual error in its only example: at 10% compounded annually, $110 grows to **$121**, not $120. $120 is simple interest, which is exactly the concept it was meant to contrast, so the error defeats the purpose of the explanation. Response A's math is correct, but it breaks two instructions: it is about 148 words (limit: 100), and it uses terms like "principal" and "APY" that a 12-year-old would not know. Because accuracy outranks style and length in most rating rubrics, A is preferable. An ideal answer would combine B's tone and length with A's correct example.

**Why it's strong:** it picks a side, names the decisive factor (factual accuracy), points to the exact error, credits and faults each response specifically, and says what "ideal" would look like.

**Common mistakes:** choosing B because "it's more kid-friendly" without checking the math; saying "both are fine"; vague reasons ("A is more detailed").
:::

## Mock Assessment 2: Write an ideal response under constraints

::: {.sample}
**Prompt:** "I just got laid off. Give me a checklist for my first 48 hours. Max 8 bullets, plain language, US-specific."
:::

::: {.qa}
**Strong answer:**

- File for unemployment in the state where you worked, today if possible. Benefits usually start from your filing date.
- Get your final paycheck details, severance terms, and any agreement *in writing*. Don't sign a release until you understand it.
- Ask HR how and when your health coverage ends. Compare COBRA with a HealthCare.gov plan (a layoff opens a special enrollment period).
- Download your pay stubs, W-2s, performance reviews, and contacts you're allowed to keep before your access ends.
- Ask about unused PTO payout and 401(k) options. Don't cash out retirement funds without checking the tax penalties.
- Update your LinkedIn and resume with your most recent results while they're fresh.
- Tell 10 people you trust that you're looking, and name the role you want.
- Make a 30-day budget and list which bills can be paused or reduced.

**Why it's strong:** it meets every constraint (8 bullets, plain words, US-specific), puts the time-sensitive steps first, is accurate (filing date matters; a layoff triggers special enrollment), and adds practical caution without giving legal advice.
:::

## Mock Assessment 3: Fact-check this paragraph

::: {.sample}
**Paragraph:** "Independent contractors receive a Form W-2 from each client at the end of the year. They pay self-employment tax of 15.3%, which covers Social Security and Medicare, and the Social Security portion applies to all net earnings with no upper limit. Quarterly estimated tax payments are generally due in April, June, September, and January. The Fair Labor Standards Act guarantees overtime pay to independent contractors who work more than 40 hours a week."

**Task:** Identify and correct every error.
:::

::: {.qa}
**Strong answer:** There are three errors. (1) Contractors receive **Form 1099-NEC** (or 1099-K from payment platforms), not a W-2, which is for employees. (2) The Social Security portion (12.4%) applies only up to an **annual wage base limit**, which is adjusted every year; the Medicare portion (2.9%) has no cap. (3) The FLSA's overtime protections cover **employees**, not independent contractors. **Correct as written:** the 15.3% combined self-employment tax rate, and the four estimated-payment months (generally April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year).

**Why it's strong:** it finds all the errors, confirms what is correct (graders penalize "finding" errors that aren't there), and gives the precise correction.
:::

## Mock Assessment 4: Coding evaluation

::: {.sample}
**Task:** The function should return the average of a list of numbers, or `None` for an empty list. Find the bug(s), fix them, and give two test cases.

```python
def average(nums):
    total = 0
    for i in range(1, len(nums)):
        total += nums[i]
    return total / len(nums)
```
:::

::: {.qa}
**Strong answer:** Two bugs. (1) `range(1, len(nums))` skips the first element, so `average([2, 4])` returns 2.0 instead of 3.0. (2) An empty list raises `ZeroDivisionError` instead of returning `None`.

```python
def average(nums):
    if not nums:
        return None
    return sum(nums) / len(nums)

assert average([2, 4]) == 3.0
assert average([]) is None
```

**Why it's strong:** it states each bug with a concrete failing input, gives a minimal idiomatic fix, and adds tests for the normal case and the edge case.
:::

## Mock Assessment 5: Search relevance rating

::: {.sample}
**Query:** "remote customer service jobs texas"

Rate each result **Highly relevant / Somewhat relevant / Not relevant** and justify in one sentence.

1. A careers page listing "Work-from-home Customer Service Advisor — must reside in TX, FL or GA."
2. A blog post titled "10 Tips for Great Customer Service."
3. A job listing for "On-site Customer Service Rep — Dallas, TX office."
:::

::: {.qa}
**Strong answer:** (1) **Highly relevant.** It is a remote customer-service job open to Texas residents, matching all three parts of the query. (2) **Not relevant.** It is about customer service skills, not jobs, and has no remote or location component. (3) **Somewhat relevant.** It matches "customer service" and "Texas" but fails the "remote" intent, which is central to the query.

**Why it's strong:** it breaks the query into its parts (job type, remote, location) and rates consistently against them, which is what rater guidelines reward.
:::

## Mock Assessment 6: AI interview (expert/resume-based)

AI interviewers on Mercor, Alignerr, and micro1 typically ask you to go deep on your own experience, explain a concept in your field, and judge or improve an answer. Practice these aloud with a 2-minute timer:

1. "Walk me through the most complex project on your resume. What was your specific role?"
2. "Explain [a core concept in your field] to a smart non-expert."
3. "Here is an answer an AI gave about [topic]. What is wrong with it, and how would you fix it?"
4. "How do you check that your own work is correct before you submit it?"
5. "Tell me about a time you found an error others missed."

::: {.qa}
**Example strong answer to Q1 (laid-off healthcare billing specialist):** "At [Clinic Group] I led the cleanup of a claims backlog after an EHR migration. About 2,300 claims were stuck because diagnosis codes didn't map correctly to the new system. My role was to define the mapping rules for the top 40 denial reasons, train three coworkers, and audit a sample every week. I built a spreadsheet that flagged mismatched ICD-10 codes before submission. Over four months, our first-pass acceptance rate went from about 78% to 93%, and the backlog cleared. The lesson I'd bring to evaluating AI answers is that small coding errors look plausible, so I verify each one against the source rule rather than trusting how it reads."

**Why it's strong:** clear ownership ("my role was…"), precise domain terms, real numbers, a method, and a bridge to the AI-evaluation job. *Use your own true details. The numbers here are illustrative.*
:::

## Mock Assessment 7: Customer service chat simulation

::: {.sample}
**Customer:** "This is the THIRD time my order is late. I want a refund NOW or I'm disputing the charge."
:::

::: {.qa}
**Strong reply:** "I'm sorry. Three late deliveries is frustrating, and I'd be upset too. I've pulled up your order #[ID]. It's currently at [status]. I can do one of two things right now: issue a full refund to your original payment method today (it usually posts in 3–5 business days), or ship a replacement with free expedited delivery arriving [date]. Which would you prefer?"

**Why it's strong:** it acknowledges the emotion without arguing, shows you took action, offers concrete choices within policy, and ends with a clear question. Assessments score empathy, ownership, accuracy, and resolution.
:::

## Most common reasons people fail

1. **Missing an instruction** (word limit, format, "explain why").
2. **Vague justifications** ("A is better because it's clearer").
3. **Inventing errors** in fact-checks, or missing the decisive one.
4. **Using AI tools** on the assessment.
5. **Rushing.** Speed is rarely scored at the entry gate; quality is.
6. **A generic resume** that gives the AI interviewer nothing specific to ask about.
7. **Poor setup**: noise, bad lighting, an unstable connection during video interviews.
8. **Applying to roles outside your verifiable expertise.**

## Preparation plan (2–3 hours, once)

::: {.checklist}
- [ ] Run prompt **P16** (Assessment Practice Generator) and complete two full practice sets.

- [ ] Write out your 3 resume stories using STAR (prompt **P15**).
- [ ] Read any public guidelines for your target rater program (search "[platform] rater guidelines").
- [ ] Test webcam, mic, and lighting; do Mercor's practice interview if you're applying there.
- [ ] Prepare a quiet 90-minute block for the real assessment.
:::

**Most assessments test one skill:** careful instruction-following plus visible reasoning.

**Practice both with prompts P14–P16 before you take a one-shot test.**
:::
